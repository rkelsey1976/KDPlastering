Not every rendering job is a whole house. Plenty of the work we do is a
single elevation — the weather side that has taken forty years of
south-westerlies while the other three walls are fine, or an extension
that needs bringing in line with the original house. The question is
whether doing one wall is a sensible repair or a job you will regret in
two years.

Here is how we think about it.

## Why one wall costs more per square metre

The instinct is that a quarter of the house should cost a quarter of the
price. It does not, and the reason is scaffold. A single gable elevation
still needs a full scaffold run, still needs the team mobilised, still
needs the same delivery of materials and the same site setup. Spread that
fixed cost over 40 square metres instead of 160 and the rate per square
metre roughly doubles. A single elevation is still good value for a
genuine repair — it is just not a proportional fraction of a full render.

## When a single elevation is the right call

- **One weather-beaten face.** If the north or south-west elevation has
  failed and the rest is sound, there is no reason to strip good render
  off three walls to match it.
- **An extension or a porch.** New work that was left in bare block or a
  different finish, and just needs pulling into line with the main house.
- **Storm or impact damage.** A section knocked off by a fallen branch or
  a delivery lorry, where the rest of the wall is fine.
- **A wall that was patched badly by someone else** and now needs doing
  properly.

## When it is a false economy

- **The other elevations are the same age and the same system.** If the
  west wall has gone, the others are usually two or three winters behind
  it. Doing one now and the rest separately means paying for scaffold and
  mobilisation twice, and it works out dearer than doing the lot in one
  visit.
- **You cannot live with a colour step.** Which brings us to the real
  problem.

## The colour-match problem

Render colour is never a perfect match across jobs done at different
times. Through-colour renders are mixed in batches, the surface weathers,
and a fresh elevation next to a ten-year-old one will show a line at the
corner — usually a shade or two lighter and cleaner. On a rear elevation
nobody sees, that does not matter. On the front of the house it can look
worse than the failed render did.

There are ways to manage it: taking the new render to a natural break
point like a corner or a downpipe rather than stopping mid-wall, choosing
a colour that the weathered render will move towards rather than away
from, or accepting that the front elevation is done as a pair with
whichever wall it turns the corner into. We will talk this through on the
survey — it is the single thing that most often changes a one-wall job
into a two-wall job.

## A rule of thumb

If the elevation that has failed is at the back, or it is genuinely a
one-off (damage, an extension), do the one wall. If it is at the front, or
the other elevations are the same age and system, price both options — one
elevation now versus the whole house — and compare them properly. The
gap is often smaller than people expect once the second lot of scaffold
is in the figures.

## What to ask for in the quote

A single-elevation quote should still spell out what happens to the
existing render on that wall, how the new work meets the old at the
corners, and what the warranty covers on a partial job. If it is vague on
the join, that is where it will go wrong.
