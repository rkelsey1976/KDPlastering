Georgian and early-Victorian houses in Bath are built differently from
anything that went up after about 1920. Solid stone walls, lime mortar, no
cavity, no damp-proof course. They were designed to get wet and then dry
out again — the whole wall breathes. Put the wrong render on one and you
stop it breathing, and the damage goes inward.

This is the single most important decision on a period Bath render, so it
is worth understanding rather than leaving to whoever quotes cheapest.

## Why the wall needs to breathe

A solid lime wall manages moisture by letting it pass through. Rain soaks
into the outer face, and in dry weather it evaporates back out. Lime
mortar and lime render are soft and porous enough to let that happen. As
long as the wall can dry outward faster than it wets, it stays sound and
the inside stays dry.

## What cement render does

Sand-and-cement render, and most standard polymer renders, are hard and
largely vapour-closed. On a cavity-walled 1970s house that is fine. On a
solid lime wall it is a problem:

- Water still gets into the wall — through cracks, around windows, up from
  the ground — but now it cannot evaporate outward through the render.
- It moves inward instead, and shows up as damp patches and peeling paint
  on the inside walls.
- Trapped moisture freezes in winter and spalls the stone and the lime
  mortar **behind** the render, where you cannot see it until the render
  comes off.
- The cement render itself, being rigid, cracks as the soft wall moves,
  and the cracks let in more water.

Ten to fifteen years of that and the bill is not a re-render, it is a
re-render plus masonry repair.

## Lime render — the traditional answer

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| Fully breathable — matches how the wall works | Slower to apply, longer project |
| Soft and flexible — moves with the wall, does not crack | Weather-sensitive to lay; no frost, no baking sun |
| Sympathetic finish, what a conservation officer wants | More expensive per m² |
| Repairable in patches for decades | Needs occasional limewash / maintenance |
| No consent friction on a listed elevation | Narrower colour range, softer look |

Lime is the right answer for a listed Georgian elevation, for a wall
that is already in poor condition, and for anyone who wants the building
done properly for the next fifty years.

## Lime-compatible modern systems — the middle path

Some through-colour silicone and mineral renders are formulated to be
vapour-open enough to sit on a lime substrate. They are not the same as
true lime, but they breathe far better than cement, they are more durable
and quicker to apply than lime, and they come in a full colour range.

They are a fair compromise on:

- Victorian and Edwardian properties, which are often a bit more forgiving
  than true Georgian
- rear and side elevations that are not in public view
- houses that are not listed, where the conservation officer is content
  with a breathable modern finish

They are **not** the answer on a listed principal elevation, or on a wall
that is holding a lot of damp and needs the maximum breathability it can
get.

## How to decide

1. **Is it listed, or on a sensitive conservation-area elevation?** If
   yes, assume lime, and confirm with BANES.
2. **What condition is the wall in?** A wall that is already damp or
   spalling needs the most breathable option available — lime.
3. **Is the elevation on show?** Front elevations lean towards lime for
   the look as much as the physics; hidden elevations have more latitude.
4. **What is the budget and timescale?** Lime costs more and takes
   longer. That is real, and it is worth it on the right wall.

## One thing not to do

Do not let anyone put standard sand-and-cement or a standard polymer
render on a solid lime wall because it is cheaper and faster. It is the
most common mistake on period Bath houses and the most expensive one to
undo. If a quote does not say what system it is using and why, ask —
and if the answer is "it'll be fine", get another quote.
