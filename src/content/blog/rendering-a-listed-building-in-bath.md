Most rendering work in Bath comes with a layer that a Bristol job does not:
consent. The city centre is a World Heritage Site, large parts of it are a
conservation area, and a good share of the housing is listed. None of that
stops you rendering your property — it changes what you can use and how you
have to go about it.

Here is how it actually works.

## First, find out what you are dealing with

Two separate things matter, and a property can be subject to both:

- **Listed status.** Search the National Heritage List for England, or ask
  Bath & North East Somerset (BANES) planning. If the building is listed,
  the listing covers the whole thing — front, back, and often the
  boundary walls — not just the bit facing the street.
- **Conservation area.** Most of central Bath, and many of the Georgian
  and Victorian suburbs, sit inside one. Some also have an Article 4
  Direction, which removes permitted-development rights that would apply
  elsewhere.

We check both before quoting, because they decide the specification.

## When you need Listed Building Consent

The rule of thumb: **like-for-like repair usually does not need consent;
changing the character does.**

| What you are doing | Consent likely? |
| --- | --- |
| Repairing failed lime render with matching lime render | Often not — but confirm with BANES |
| Replacing cement render with a breathable system | Usually yes |
| Rendering a currently bare-stone or brick elevation | Yes |
| Changing colour or texture on a visible elevation | Yes |
| Rendering a rear elevation that already carries render | Case by case |

Doing work that needed consent and did not get it is a criminal offence,
and it follows the building — it becomes the next owner's problem at sale.
It is not a corner worth cutting.

## What conservation area status changes

If the building is not listed but sits in a conservation area, you
generally have more freedom, but the council can still control external
render through an Article 4 Direction or by requiring planning permission
for the change. The test the officer applies is whether the work
preserves or enhances the character of the area. A sympathetic
lime-coloured finish on a Georgian terrace passes that test; a bright
white polymer render on the same terrace does not.

## Which systems get approved

For solid lime-built walls — which is most of pre-1900 Bath — the
approved options are breathable ones:

- **Lime render.** The traditional answer, fully vapour-open, sympathetic
  to the stone. Slower to apply and weather-sensitive, but it is what a
  conservation officer wants to see on a listed Georgian elevation.
- **Lime-compatible silicone or mineral systems.** Some modern
  through-colour renders are breathable enough to sit on a lime substrate
  and are accepted on less sensitive elevations and on later (Victorian,
  Edwardian) properties. Whether one is acceptable on your building is a
  conversation to have with the officer, not an assumption to make.

Standard sand-and-cement and standard polymer renders are almost always
refused on solid lime walls, and for good reason — they trap moisture in
the wall and damage the masonry behind them.

## How the process runs

1. **Site visit.** We survey the substrate, work out what is repair and
   what is replacement, and identify what will need consent.
2. **Pre-application advice.** For anything borderline, we or your
   architect put the proposal to BANES informally before a formal
   application. It saves a refused application later.
3. **The application.** Listed Building Consent applications take around
   eight weeks. Drawings, a heritage statement and a specification of the
   render system go in together.
4. **The work.** Only once consent is granted. We work to the approved
   specification, and the conservation officer may want to inspect.

## What we handle and what we do not

We handle the render specification, the substrate assessment, and the
practical side of an application — the system data sheets, the method,
the justification for the choice. A full Listed Building Consent
application, especially one with drawings, usually wants an architect or
a heritage consultant, and we will tell you at the site visit whether
your job needs one or whether it is straightforward enough to submit
directly.

## The short version

If your Bath property is listed or in a conservation area, budget for the
consent process and the eight weeks it takes, and do not let anyone quote
you a standard cement render for a solid lime wall. The premium on a Bath
heritage job — typically 15 to 25 percent over an equivalent Bristol
render — is the consent work, the lime materials, and the slower,
specialist application. It buys you a wall that lasts and a sale that
does not fall through on a survey.
